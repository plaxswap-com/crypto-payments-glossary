Crypto payments glossary for merchants

The words that come up when a shop, freelancer or online service starts taking crypto, explained in plain language. Each entry says what the term means and why it matters when money is on the line.

Sections: Coins and networks · Checkout · Payment outcomes · Integration · Fees, custody and payouts

Coins and networks

Blockchain network
The system that records and settles a transaction, such as Bitcoin, Ethereum, Tron or BNB Chain. A payment only counts if it is sent on the network the checkout asked for.
Stablecoin
A token designed to track the value of a currency, usually the US dollar. USDT and USDC are the best known. Merchants like them because the amount received does not swing the way Bitcoin's price does.
USDT
Tether's dollar stablecoin. It exists on several networks at once, so "USDT" alone is not enough information: the customer also has to know which network to use.
TRC-20
The token standard on Tron. USDT on Tron is a TRC-20 token. Transfers are usually fast and the fee is paid in TRX or with Tron's energy and bandwidth resources.
ERC-20
The token standard on Ethereum. USDT and USDC on Ethereum are ERC-20 tokens. The network fee is paid in ETH and can be high when Ethereum is busy, which matters most on small orders.
Native coin
The coin a network uses for its own fees: BTC on Bitcoin, ETH on Ethereum, TRX on Tron, BNB on BNB Chain. A wallet needs some of it to move tokens on that network.
Wallet address
The string a payment is sent to. Addresses look different on each network, and coins sent to the wrong network or a mistyped address are usually lost.
Memo or destination tag
An extra field some networks and exchanges use to tell payments to the same address apart. If a checkout shows one, the customer must include it.

Checkout

Crypto payment gateway
A service that lets a business receive crypto. It shows the customer what to send, watches the network for the payment and tells the business when it has settled.
Hosted checkout
A payment page run by the gateway. Your server creates a payment and sends the customer to that page, so you do not have to build address display, QR codes or payment tracking yourself.
Payment link
A single checkout URL for a set price and reference. It works without a website: you can send it in a chat or email, or show it as a QR code. For business clients it can act as a crypto invoice.
Quote
The exact amount the customer must send in the coin and network they picked, worked out from the order price and current rates. Quotes usually hold for a limited time.
Price cushion
A small extra amount added when the customer's coin has to be converted, so that a price move while the coins confirm still covers the order. Any amount left over goes to the merchant.
Payment expiry
The time limit on a payment request. After it, the quote is no longer valid. Gateways differ in what they do with coins that arrive late, so check the rules before you rely on them.

Payment outcomes

Confirmation
Each new block added on top of the block that holds a transaction. More confirmations make a reversal less likely. A Bitcoin block arrives about every ten minutes on average, so Bitcoin payments take longer to settle than payments on faster networks.
Pending payment
A payment request that is waiting for the customer to send coins.
Confirming payment
Coins have arrived but are still being confirmed or checked. Do not ship goods at this stage.
Paid
The customer sent the amount asked and the payment settled. This is the status to wait for before you deliver.
Partial payment (underpayment)
Less than the amount asked arrived. This often happens when a customer sends from an exchange that takes its withdrawal fee out of the amount. Decide in advance whether you ask for the rest, refund, or accept the shortfall.
Overpayment
More than the amount asked arrived. Gateways usually pass the extra on and flag the payment, and the merchant decides whether to refund the difference.
Late payment
Coins that arrive after the payment request expired. Some gateways still credit them and mark them as late, so your system should not treat "expired" as final without checking.
Refund
Sending coins back to the customer. A crypto payment cannot be pulled back the way a card charge can, so refunds are a new transaction and need a correct address from the customer.

Integration

API key
A secret your server uses to create and read payments. Keep it on the server, never in browser code, and use separate keys for testing and live payments.
Test mode
A way to run the whole payment flow without moving real coins, so you can check how your shop handles each outcome before going live.
Webhook
A message the gateway sends to a URL on your server when a payment changes status. It lets your shop update orders without polling.
Webhook signature
A code sent with each webhook, usually an HMAC made with a shared secret. Your server checks it to prove the message really came from the gateway and was not changed.
Idempotency key
A unique value sent with a request, such as your order number, so that retrying the same request does not create a second payment.
Plugin
Ready-made code that adds a gateway to a platform such as WooCommerce, WHMCS or XenForo, so you can accept crypto without writing an integration.

Fees, custody and payouts

Network fee
The fee paid to the blockchain network to process a transaction, in that network's native coin. It goes to the network, not to the gateway, and it changes with how busy the network is.
Platform fee
The gateway's own charge, usually a percentage of each payment. Check who pays it, the customer or the merchant, because that changes your pricing.
Custody
Who holds the coins. With a custodial gateway, payments go to wallets the gateway runs and you see a balance you can withdraw. With a non-custodial setup, payments go straight to addresses you control and you manage the keys yourself.
Merchant balance
The record of coins a custodial gateway holds for you, usually kept per coin and network. Treat it as money in transit and withdraw on a regular schedule.
Withdrawal
Moving coins from your gateway balance to your own wallet. Expect a gateway fee and a network fee, and check both before you approve.
Recovery phrase
The list of words that can restore a self-custody wallet. Anyone who has it controls the coins, and if it is lost nobody can recover them.
Compliance screening
Checks a gateway runs on payments and wallets, such as sanctions screening. A payment held for review is not final until the check ends, and gateways are not available in every country.

An example: how Plaxswap uses these terms

To see the terms in one real setup, take Plaxswap, a crypto service whose payment gateway offers payment links, a hosted checkout with a REST API, and plugins for WooCommerce, WHMCS and XenForo. Its API lists the statuses pending, confirming, paid, partially_paid, expired, cancelled and failed, with flags for late and overpaid payments, and it sends signed webhooks. Coins that arrive up to 24 hours after a payment expired are still passed on and marked late.

On cost, the customer pays a 1.5% platform fee plus the conversion and network costs shown in their quote, with a 0.5% price cushion when their coin is converted. Confirmed payments go to a merchant balance that Plaxswap holds until the merchant withdraws, and each withdrawal costs 0.3% plus the network fee. The setup is described on the Plaxswap merchants page, and the full list of charges is on the Plaxswap fees page. Whatever gateway you pick, run a test payment for each outcome before you take real orders.
